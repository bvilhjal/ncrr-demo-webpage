Research

Psychiatric epidemiology on nationwide Danish data — from registers to genes.

Why Denmark?

Denmark's distinctive registers make it one of the leading countries in the world for register-based research. Register data covering the entire Danish population have been gathered over decades, largely for administrative purposes, and Danish legislation permits researchers to use them for research of general relevance and importance. The registers enable study of large nationwide samples that are less biased than most other samples: healthcare in Denmark is free for everyone, and there are essentially no private psychiatric hospitals.

NCRR research has advanced epidemiological research on schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, autism and ADHD, as well as combined disorder studies, mortality and suicide, eating disorders, psychopharmacology and more. Over the past decade, genetic and other biological data have been incorporated — bio-banked samples enable large-scale population-based genetic studies and research into gene–environment interactions linked to psychiatric disorders. The work is interdisciplinary, involving collaboration with research groups and frequent interaction with leading epidemiologists in Denmark and abroad. Our aim is to improve life for people with mental disorders and, potentially, to find future prevention methods for severe mental illness.

CIRRAU

Established 2012. Centre for Integrated Register-based Research, enabling interdisciplinary register-based research across social sciences, health sciences and elements of natural sciences. CIRRAU researchers maintain a database at Statistics Denmark, strengthened by linkage to biological material and genetic information on 80,000 Danes from the Danish Neonatal Screening Bank at Statens Serum Institut. Its data solution underpins register-based analyses in programmes funded by the Lundbeck Foundation, TrygFonden, the Stanley Medical Research Institute, the EU, the Simon Foundation and NordForsk.

Lundbeck Foundation initiative

iPSYCH

The Lundbeck Foundation Initiative for Integrative Psychiatric Research: a partnership between Aarhus University, the University of Copenhagen, the Central Region of Denmark, the Capital Region of Denmark and the Danish State Serum Institute, funded by three Lundbeck Foundation grants (121M DKK in 2012 and 120M DKK each in 2015 and 2018).

Six research groups spanning medicine, molecular biology, bioinformatics, statistics and psychology study autism, ADHD, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and depression through environmental, genetic and psychological risk factors from fetal life through adulthood. More than 150 researchers take part, with international partners including the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, deCode Genetics (Iceland) and the Genetic Biobank of the Faroe Islands.

The iPSYCH2012 case-cohort sample is one of the project's major achievements: it covers the entire Danish population born 1981–2005 — 1,472,762 persons — and provides a long-term foundation for studying environmental and genetic risk factors for severe mental disorders.

European Stanley Research Centre

NCRR participates in the Stanley Program for Epidemiology, Prevention and Treatment of Schizophrenia (SPECTS), supported by the Stanley Medical Research Institute. SPECTS brings together eight international leading laboratories studying gene–environment interactions that may raise the risk of psychiatric disorders, and is dedicated to developing biomarkers to predict onset and progression of mental illness. Partners besides NCRR: Johns Hopkins University, Karolinska Institute, WRAIR, University of Cambridge, Sheppard Pratt, University of Michigan and University of Pittsburgh.

Niels Bohr Professorship

In 2016, Professor John McGrath (University of Queensland) was awarded a Niels Bohr Professorship funded by a 30M DKK grant from the Danish National Research Foundation. The professorship follows up on the 2010 finding — based on Danish registers and collaboration with Professor Preben Bo Mortensen, NCRR and Statens Serum Institut — linking neonatal vitamin D levels to risk of mental disorders in offspring, and supports new methods in psychiatric epidemiology. Close collaboration with iPSYCH, the University of Queensland, the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation and the WHO World Mental Health Survey Initiative at Harvard University.

NCRR research

The academic team — professors, senior researchers, postdocs, PhD students and research assistants — works to identify and pursue ways of using the information stored in the Danish population-based registers. Questions we address include:

  • How many people have a particular mental disorder?
  • Do mental disorders occur differently in men and women?
  • When do different mental disorders emerge across the lifespan?
  • Which risk factors are inherited from parents, and which are encountered in life — trauma, stress, low parental vitamin D?
  • How disabling are mental disorders, and how can we best measure this burden?

While the causes of most mental disorders remain unknown, biological, psychological and environmental factors can all contribute, and most disorders arise from a combination rather than a single cause. Our core hypothesis is that environmental risk factors contribute to the etiology of a range of mental disorders through their impact on neurodevelopment — in fetal life, infancy and childhood — and that these early exposures may interact with later risks such as social adversity, physical illness or genetic susceptibility. Combining biological, health and social data is expected to reveal interacting genetic and environmental causes of mental disorders.

Research themes

Schizophrenia ▾

Schizophrenia is a poorly understood mental illness that impacts perception and cognition and affects roughly one in 100 people, causing substantial disability, suffering and major societal economic costs. Danish registers have enabled NCRR researchers to document risk factors including psychiatric family history, urban birth, paternal age, lifetime risk, infections, socioeconomic adversity and neonatal vitamin D deficiency; outcomes studied include treatment-resistant schizophrenia, suicide, excess mortality and pharmacological treatment. Through iPSYCH and international collaboration we have identified genetic variants that may raise schizophrenia risk, illuminating the biological processes involved in the development of schizophrenia and other mental disorders.

Current research questions

  • Do neonatal vitamin D levels linked to schizophrenia risk also connect to other mental illnesses?
  • What genetic architecture increases schizophrenia risk?
  • How does schizophrenia relate to risks of physical illnesses (obesity, diabetes, infections)?
  • Can genes be identified that predict schizophrenia risk or a chronic course?
  • What genetic overlap exists with affective disorders, autism and ADHD?

Mortality and suicide ▾

People with severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder die 15–20 years earlier than the general population. Four factors explain this: suboptimal health-related behaviour, adverse medication effects, elevated suicide risk, and high somatic and mental comorbidity combined with insufficient treatment. Although excess mortality from accidents and suicide is well known, the primary explanation for early death is physical illness treated poorly or too late. Danish register access lets us track decades of data to study excess mortality and propose causes — for example, NCRR studies show that people with mental disorders are less likely to receive heart disease treatment or cardiovascular medication despite greater vulnerability. Recently we have expanded into large-scale population-based genetic studies and gene–environment interaction research, and — through the Niels Bohr professorship with Professor John McGrath — into the Global Burden of Disease project and the WHO World Mental Health Survey Initiative.

Current research questions

  • How do genes affect the high mortality rate among people with schizophrenia?
  • Do genes that increase risk of schizophrenia also increase risk of early death?
  • How does air pollution affect mortality?
  • How does the presence of multiple mental illnesses affect mortality?
  • Which causes of death explain excess mortality — suicide, cardiovascular disease, cancer?

Affective disorders (bipolar, depression, and anxiety) ▾

Affective disorders — mainly depression, bipolar disorder and anxiety disorder — involve abnormalities of emotional state and carry consequences such as functional impairment, disability, lost work productivity and increased need of health services. The Danish registers are a remarkable epidemiological resource here: longitudinal data on the entire population covering psychiatric and other medical diagnoses, demographics, income and labour market affiliation allow investigation of questions difficult or impossible in other samples, such as determinants of rare outcomes in bipolar disorder and long-term causes and outcomes of depression. One recent study showed that patients with the same severe depression diagnosis receive treatment of very different duration in the Danish psychiatric system; another, covering roughly one million Danish children, found that maternal antidepressant use during pregnancy increases the risk of offspring later being diagnosed with psychiatric illness. Current research explores how genetic risk and early adverse life events interact, to improve prediction, prevention and treatment.

Current research questions

  • To which extent does genetic risk affect the onset, course and outcome of affective disorders within the Danish population?
  • How do early adverse life events interact with genetic risks associated with affective disorders?
  • Do genes associated with increased risk of schizophrenia also predispose to affective disorders such as depression?

Autism ▾

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviour. Symptoms emerge in the first years of life; although treatment can improve functioning, ASD is generally lifelong, can be very disabling and carries major economic costs. ASD affects 1–2% of children, yet remains rare compared with chronic conditions like asthma or obesity — large population-based studies with complete pregnancy and early-life data are needed to pinpoint risk factors. NCRR's data resources support studies of family and pregnancy risk factors (parental age, occupational exposures during pregnancy, obstetric and newborn complications, family medical history, urbanicity), time trends in incidence, antidepressant use in pregnancy, and family history of related disorders. NCRR houses what we believe is the largest assembly of genetic, biological and longitudinal data on persons with ASD in the world.

Current research questions

  • How do genes influence ASD risk from exposures like traffic-related air pollution — and do ASD and ADHD show the same or different patterns?
  • Do pregnancy environmental factors that alter gene expression affect ASD risk?
  • How do genes interact with maternal smoking or diabetes?
  • What genetic overlap exists between ASD, schizophrenia and ADHD, and which biologic pathways are shared?

ADHD ▾

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is a highly prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder and the most common mental disorder in children, bringing severe impairments across the lifespan and many adverse outcomes. Danish registers permit long prospective follow-up of the entire population with no attrition — critical for ADHD, where follow-up studies typically suffer high dropout; register studies can retain 100% of a sample even after 30 years. NCRR researchers were the first to show that ADHD is linked to increased risk of premature death, and later work found elevated risk of other mental disorders (substance use, affective disorders, psychosis) and, in children, injuries, emergency ward visits, teenage parenthood and adult criminality. We pioneered large female ADHD samples, illuminating sex differences. A meta-analysis of 20 twin studies estimated ADHD heritability at 75%, and NCRR contributed to identifying SNPs in 12 loci genome-wide significantly associated with ADHD, enabling direct measurement of genetic liability via a polygenic risk score.

Current research questions

  • Does exposure to air pollutants early in life increase the risk of ADHD?
  • Are xenobiotic and geogenic substances in drinking water risk factors for ADHD?
  • How do genetics and air- and drinking-water-borne substances interact in the etiology of ADHD?
  • How well do genetics and adversities in parent–child interactions predict adult adverse health outcomes of childhood ADHD?

Psychopharmacology ▾

Patients with mental disorders are often treated with psychotropic drugs. This field examines the impact of different drugs on patients with mental disorders — how compounds alter behaviour through thought patterns and emotions, and potential long-term physical effects. Detecting rare events requires large datasets, so NCRR uses Danish register data to study treatment courses and outcomes; the EU-funded projects CRESTAR and PROTECT let researchers work relatively independently of drug-company funding. In CRESTAR (ended 31 October 2015), researchers analysed samples from more than 11,000 patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia plus nearly 25,000 controls, identifying 10 genetic loci linked to treatment resistance and clozapine failure. PROTECT compares and develops pharmacoepidemiological methods to quantify and communicate treatment risks and benefits, with NCRR leading in combining register, genetic and other biological data. Other areas include anti-depressive treatment response, sickness absence among antidepressant users, and pharmacological treatment of dementia patients.

Current research questions

  • How does clozapine treatment affect mortality and self-harm in individuals with treatment-resistant schizophrenia?
  • What is the prevalence of drug prescriptions for high-risk pregnancies among Danish women with a psychiatric history, compared with Danish women generally?

HOPE — postpartum depression ▾

In Denmark, around 8–9,000 new mothers develop a postpartum depression each year — roughly 10–15% of women who give birth. Symptoms include sadness, lack of desire and joy for the child, appetite loss and sleeping difficulties; in rare cases there may be thoughts of self-harm or of harming the child. The disorder harms the mother, the child and the family, and there are currently no tools to identify which women will develop it.

The HOPE project gathers data on women who have recently or previously given birth, identifies risk factors for postpartum depression and postpartum psychosis, and studies the consequences of these disorders, matching data against Danish health registers covering the mothers, their children and other women. The goal is to explain why some women develop the condition while others do not, and to build a risk prediction tool that lets health visitors offer intervention or early treatment to high-risk women.

Other projects ▾

The centre draws on extensive Danish register data to study the long-term causes and outcomes of severe mental disorders — documented epidemiological factors include urban birth, paternal age, family psychiatric history, lifetime risk, infections, neonatal vitamin D deficiency, socio-economic adversity, treatment-resistant schizophrenia and pharmacological treatment. Beyond the core psychiatric work, researchers pursue register-based projects on other public-health topics, such as the health consequences of air pollution, links between schooling, education and social mobility, and connections between lifestyle and health problems including cancer. The iPSYCH2012 sample offers a continuous reliability measure, boosting the ability to merge genetic, environmental and phenotypic data; related Danish projects now share iPSYCH's framework, covering anorexia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, conduct disorder and hyperkinetic conduct disorder.
